What are cyclones?

A tropical cyclone is a huge rotating storm that forms over warm ocean water. It brings strong winds, heavy rain and a rise in sea level called storm surge. Hurricanes, typhoons and cyclones are the same kind of storm, with different names in different places.16

Same storm, different names

cyclone

Used in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific. This includes the Bay of Bengal, off Myanmar.

Where the storm forms

The storm gets one of these names once its winds reach 119 km/h (74 mph). Myanmar lies on the Bay of Bengal, so storms here are called cyclones.

How a cyclone forms

Warm sea water heats the air above it. That air rises, more air rushes in underneath, and Earth’s rotation makes the whole system spin. Step through it to see how.

1. Warm sea water

A cyclone needs warm ocean water, about 26.5 °C or warmer.23 The warm water heats the air above it and adds water vapour.

2. Rising air, falling pressure

The warm, moist air rises. With less air left near the surface, the air pressure there drops.

3. Air rushes in

Air from nearby, where the pressure is higher, rushes in. It warms and rises too. High up, the water vapour cools and turns into clouds, which releases heat and keeps powering the storm.

4. Spin and the eye

Earth’s rotation bends the winds, so they swirl around the centre, counter-clockwise in the northern hemisphere. The middle becomes a calm, mostly cloud-free eye.

A cyclone keeps growing while it has warm water to feed on. Over land, or over cooler water, it loses that fuel and gradually weakens.23

How big can one get?

2,220 km across

Typhoon Tip in 1979 is the largest tropical cyclone on record. It was nearly half the size of the continental United States.06

What a cyclone can do

A cyclone is dangerous in three ways at once. How bad the damage is depends on the storm’s strength, its path, and how prepared the people in its way are.14

  • Strong wind

    Winds are strongest near the centre. They can rip roofs off, snap trees and bring down power lines.

  • Heavy rain and floods

    Thick bands of cloud drop huge amounts of rain. Rivers and towns can flood, even far from the coast.

  • Storm surge

    Strong winds push sea water onto the land. The sea can rise by several metres and flood low coasts, so people have to leave before it arrives.

How strong can the winds get?

One common way to rate wind is the Saffir–Simpson scale, used by the US National Hurricane Center. It runs from Category 1 to 5.15

178–208 km/h

The Post-Nargis Joint Assessment reports Category 3 and winds up to about 200 km/h;18 a storm-surge study by Fritz and colleagues describes Nargis as Category 4.03 The Saffir–Simpson scale uses maximum sustained wind, and this page does not specify the averaging period for the 200 km/h figure, so treat these as source-specific classifications.

Case study: Cyclone Nargis

Cyclone Nargis struck the Ayeyarwady Delta on 2–3 May 2008, with winds of up to about 200 km/h and a storm surge of around 3.6 m (12 feet).03 The official joint assessment called it the worst natural disaster in Myanmar’s history. It killed or left missing 138,373 people.18, 04

The full toll, with the numbers for people displaced, homes destroyed and the cost of the damage, is on the History page, alongside the other cyclones that have struck Myanmar.

    Simple steps to stay safer

    Acting early makes a big difference. These are the basics.13 The Warnings and Prepare pages go further.

    • Listen to official warnings Follow radio, TV, phone alerts and local leaders, then act on what they say.
    • Pack before you need to Drinking water, food, medicines, a torch, a charged phone and important papers.
    • Leave early if the sea can reach you Storm surge needs people to move before it arrives. Know your route and shelter, and go when told to.
    • Stay away from windows If you are still indoors when the winds are dangerous, stay inside, away from windows.
    • Never go into floodwater Just six inches (about 15 cm) of fast-moving water can knock an adult over. Turn around.17
