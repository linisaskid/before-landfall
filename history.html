Myanmar has always lived with cyclones.

The Bay of Bengal sends storms to Myanmar’s coast almost every year. Most pass quietly. A few have changed whole communities. This page follows them from Akyab in 1968 to Mocha in 2023, and looks closely at Nargis, the worst of them all.

Five storms that shaped the record

Two stretches of coast take the hits: Rakhine in the north-west, and the Ayeyarwady Delta in the south-west. Every storm below arrived in April–May or in October, the seasons when storms are most likely to form over the bay and turn toward Myanmar.

  1. 1967–1968

    Akyab (now Sittwe)

    Rakhine coast

    Three storms in about a year

    Akyab was hit by cyclones in May and October 1967, and again on 10 May 1968. The 1968 storm is regarded as the worst natural disaster to strike the country in the 20th century, before Nargis. Reports of how many died range from a few hundred to more than a thousand.20, 22

  2. 29 April 2006

    Cyclone Mala

    Rakhine coast

    37 deaths

    Mala struck the west coast and knocked out electricity in affected towns. It killed 37 people.22

  3. 2–3 May 2008

    Cyclone Nargis

    Ayeyarwady Delta

    138,373 dead or missing

    The worst natural disaster in Myanmar’s history.18 A storm surge of around 3.6 m swept across the low delta.03 See the full account below.

  4. 22 October 2010

    Cyclone Giri

    Rakhine coast

    157 deaths

    Giri made landfall as an extremely severe cyclonic storm and killed 157 people. Around 70% of the city of Kyaukphyu was destroyed, and damage was estimated at US$359 million.10, 22

  5. 14 May 2023

    Cyclone Mocha

    Near Sittwe, Rakhine

    145 to 463+ deaths, depending on the source

    Mocha came ashore with sustained winds of about 195 km/h, tying Giri as one of the strongest landfalling cyclones in the satellite era. Displaced people living in camps around Sittwe were among the hardest hit,01 and the death toll is hard to confirm: ASEAN reported 145 deaths, and the National Unity Government reported at least 463.21

Death tolls for older storms and for Mocha vary between sources, because records were incomplete or access to the affected areas was limited. Where figures differ, this page gives the range.

Nargis, 2–3 May 2008

Nargis formed over the Bay of Bengal in late April and strengthened quickly. Satellites watched it for days. On 2 May it crossed the Mouths of the Irrawaddy and followed the coast north-east, passing almost directly over Yangon. Winds reached about 200 km/h, and the storm surge of around 3.6 m flooded the whole low coastal plain.18, 03

What the satellites saw afterwards

On 5 May, the satellite image shows the entire coastal plain under water. Farmland was hit especially hard. In one village about 27 km south of Yangon, a satellite photograph taken on 7 May showed that the trees and buildings along its single street had been replaced by piles of rubble, and the fields around it were submerged. Across the delta, the UN reported that 90–95% of buildings were destroyed in the worst-hit areas.11, 12

The rice bowl was ruined

The delta is Myanmar’s rice bowl. The sea water that flooded the farmland also killed over 100,000 draught animals and destroyed food and seed stocks. Survivors lost their homes and the means to grow the next harvest.08

About the images on this site

The pictures here are satellite images, which are public domain or openly licensed and show the scale of the destruction without showing the victims. Photographs taken on the ground, including aerial views of the devastated delta and the camps for displaced families, are held in the United Nations Photo archive.

What the record shows

Looking across the five storms, the same lessons come back.

  • The same coast is hit again and again Akyab was struck three times within a year in 1967–68, and Sittwe was hit again by Mocha in 2023. Families can expect to face more than one storm in their lives.
  • Water kills more than wind Over 80% of Nargis deaths were linked to the storm surge. Strong winds are frightening, but low coastal land is most at risk from the sea.
  • The most vulnerable have the least protection In 2023, people in displacement camps near Sittwe were among those who suffered most. A good plan has to include people who cannot easily move.
  • A warning has to lead to action Forecasts reached Myanmar before Nargis, but many people did not act on them, and the regional alerts did not include a surge warning. Hours of notice only help if people know what to do with them.
